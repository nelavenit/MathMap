**Definition.** Let $P$ be a set and $\leq$ be a relation on $P$. Then $\leq$ is called a <u>partial order relation</u> or just <u>order relation</u> if it is:
1. [[Reflexivity and Irreflexivity|Reflexive]]
2. [[Transitivity|Transitive]]
3. [[Symmetry and Antisymmetry|Antisymmetric]]
In other words iff $(P,\leq)$ is a [[Preorder|preordered set]] and $\leq$ is [[Symmetry and Antisymmetry|antisymmetric]].
**Remark.** The common names for $\leq$ are also <u>partial ordering</u> and <u>ordering</u>.

**Definition.** Let $P$ be a set and $\leq$ be a partial order relation on $P$. Then the pair $(P, \leq)$ is called  <u>partially ordered set</u> or <u>poset</u>, or <u>ordered set</u>, or <u>partial order</u> or just <u>order</u>.
**Remark.** In some sources (e.g. @schroderOrderedSetsIntroduction2016) ordering and order are synonyms meaning the relation, we would avoid such naming for unambiguity.
**Remark.** To avoid too many names the ones we would mostly use are "order relation" for the $\leq$, "poset" and "order" for the $(P,\leq)$.

**Definition.** Let $P$ be a set and $<$ be a relation on $P$. Then $<$ is called a <u>strict partial order relation</u> or just <u>strict order relation</u> if it is:
1. [[Reflexivity and Irreflexivity|Irreflexive]]
2. [[Transitivity|Transitive]]
**Remark.** The common names for $<$ are also <u>strict partial ordering</u> and <u>strict ordering</u>.

**Statement.** We would mostly use $\leq$, because strict and "unstrict" ordering are in one-to-one relation $\leq \equiv (< \bigcup =)$.
